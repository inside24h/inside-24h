# Pacing: "Over how long?" (2026-10-19)

This closes the open item from the sense audit of 2026-10-18 (item 2): "the model assumes everything is drunk at once".

Backups of every touched file are in `backups/20261019-142906-pace/`, under the same paths as in the repo. Nothing was deleted and nothing was committed.

## What the person sees

- A new row, **"Over how long?  All at once · 1 h · 2 h · 3 h · 4 h"**, sits in the header under the food (MealBar.tsx, `PaceBar`). It shows in every view (Simple, Full and Detailed). It appears only when there is something to spread:
  - one food with 2 or more servings (3 beers, 5 rakija, a whole pizza);
  - a meal with 2 or more drink servings (pizza + 3 beers).
- With several **alcoholic** servings, the row is highlighted, the same way "Which tea?" is, with the line "Few people drink them all in one go: pick yours." The highlight goes away after the first tap. It comes back when the person picks a new food.
- Picking a new food resets the row to "All at once". Changing an amount or adding an item to a meal keeps the chosen pace.

## Default: "All at once" (decision, with reasons)

Several people would argue for a default of about one drink every 45–60 minutes. I kept **All at once** as the default and made the control prominent instead. The reasons:

1. **The task requires that old links behave as they do today.** A paced default would change the numbers behind every existing link and every number in the sense audit.
2. **It is the cautious ceiling.** "All at once" gives the highest peak the drinks can produce. The app never shows a lower peak because of a drinking speed the person did not enter. For driving, which is the main use of these numbers, that is the right side to err on.
3. **Any assumed pace is a guess (level C).** Real evenings range from shots in 10 minutes to beers over 5 hours. No source gives a "typical" pace that we could defend.
4. **The end barely depends on the pace** (see the physics below). The answers people act on most are "When is it gone?" and "Can I drive tomorrow morning?", and the default does not mislead them. Only the peak is affected, and the screen now points to the control right there. The peak chapter says "Pick “Over how long?” …", and the gone answer says "(pick “Over how long?” to spread it)".

## Physics

### Alcohol (Widmark, zero-order)

The liver removes a fixed amount per hour once its enzyme is saturated, which is above about 0.02–0.1 ‰ (Jones 2010, PMID 20304569: Km of class I ADH is 2–10 mg/100 ml; "zero-order kinetics is adequate"). The Widmark formula used in forensic practice counts elimination "at its full rate from the start of drinking to the relevant time" (Searle 2015, PMID 24644224, full text, quoted with `epmc.py grep`). It follows that:

- the time until the alcohol is gone ≈ C0 / β, **counted from the first drink, whatever the pace**;
- the peak ≈ C0 − β × (time to the evening's peak). Spreading the drinks moves the peak later, so more has been cleared by then, and the peak is lower.

A primary study agrees: in 18 adults, 480 ml of beer drunk at 20 ml per 5 min gave less than half the blood alcohol at 75 min of the faster speeds, and by 150 min the three speeds were alike (Oshima 2022, PMID 36364765, PMC9653949, read in full).

**Model (`science/foodSim.ts`).** The amount is split into N equal rounds, one every (time ÷ N) minutes, the first at minute 0. Five beers over 3 h means one every 36 min. Each round's alcohol enters the gut at its start minute and is absorbed with the fitted rate for its drink class (Mitchell 2014). One two-compartment body and one zero-order liver handle the whole evening, so the liver works on the total.

**Hand check against the model**, default man (70 kg, 175 cm, fasted), 5 × 0.5 l beer (97.9 g, F 0.90):

| Over | Peak (model) | Peak at | Gone (model) |
|---|---|---|---|
| All at once | 1.63 ‰ | 1 h 16 | 848 min (14.1 h) |
| 1 h | 1.56 ‰ | 1 h 47 | 848 min |
| 2 h | 1.46 ‰ | 2 h 26 | 848 min |
| 3 h | 1.35 ‰ | 3 h 11 | 848 min |
| 4 h | 1.24 ‰ | 3 h 58 | 848 min |

Widmark by hand: r = 0.74 (Forrest, BMI 22.9), so C0 = 97.9 × 0.90 / (0.74 × 70) = 1.70 g/L.
- **Gone:** 1.70 / 0.12 = 14.2 h. The model gives 14.1 h at every pace.
- **Peak over 3 h:** 1.70 − 0.12 × 3.18 h = 1.32 ‰. The model gives 1.35 ‰.

Other cases:
- Ten beers: 3.44 → 2.95 ‰ (over 4 h), gone after 28.3 h at every pace.
- Three rakija: 0.79 → 0.39 ‰, gone after 6.0 h at every pace.

**The exception (handled).** When the drinks are so far apart that the blood is clear between them, the liver idles, and the end comes later than with everything at once. The text then says so: "the end comes about … later than all at once".
- `alcoholClock` now defines "gone" as after the **last** minute with alcohol. Before, it was the first minute below the cutoff after the peak. For a single curve the two are the same.
- The fast and slow livers re-run the downslope from a **pivot**. All at once, the pivot is the peak. Paced, it is the all-at-once peak minute, because the liver has been working since the first drink. Without this, a slow liver would look finished 30–45 min too early after a paced evening.

### Stomach, sugar and caffeine

- **Stomach.** Each round is a smaller meal, so on its own it would leave faster (GE12: a bigger calorie load empties slower). Overlapping rounds do slow each other, so per phase the amount that has left the stomach is
  `min(what the rounds would release on their own, what leaves when everything is had at once)` (level C).
  Without this cap, 5 colas over 1 h would deliver sugar and caffeine faster than 5 colas at once.
- **Sugar.** Each round has its own smaller and quicker curve, and the curves are added up. The dip after a sugary drink is kept only after the last round. If the result would peak higher than having it all at once, the stomach is the bottleneck, so the at-once curve stands.
  - Evidence: 50 g of glucose sipped over 3.5 h gave a similar 4-h glucose area with a flatter curve and about half the insulin (Jenkins 1990, PMID 2191884). 75 g sipped over 3.25 h flattened glucose and insulin (Grant 2026, PMID 42072154).
  - I tried an area-matched version and dropped it. The at-once curve saturates, so area-matching would shrink a cola every 48 minutes below one cola on its own.
  - Results for 5 colas: peak 3.14 at once, 1 h and 2 h; 2.62 over 3 h; 2.28 over 4 h. One cola alone gives 2.3.
- **Caffeine.** The model after the stomach is linear (espressoSim.ts). So the paced caffeine is the stomach outflow (with the cap above) convolved with one dose placed straight in the gut (`pacedCaffeine`).
  - For water-like drinks this equals the single doses added up. A test checks this to within 2% (6% in the first 30 min, because the minute grid rounds the half-time from 13.2 to 14 min).
  - Results: 4 espressos over 3 h peak at 4.53 mg/L vs 4.85 mg/L at once, and later.
  - 5 colas over 4 h peak about 6% higher than at once, and later. The reason: at once, the 700 kcal leave the stomach slowly too, and the caffeine half-life is 6 h. This is consistent within the model. It is not an error.
- **The rest.** Water, energy, protein, fat and fiber are the rounds added up. The same total arrives, only later; a test checks calories and urine at 24 h.

## Deep link

- The parameter is `over=1h|2h|3h|4h`. The parser also reads `180`, `180m` and `3h`.
- Anything that is not one of the offered choices means all at once: `7h`, `90m`, `zz`, `-1h`, or an empty value.
- Old links have no `over` and behave as before. `over` is written only when it is not 0 and the item can be paced.
- Examples: `#q=beer&n=3&over=3h`, `#m=pizza+beer*3&over=2h`.
- Compare keeps the pace for the item on screen. The share caption ends with "over 3 h".

## Open

1. **Rounds overlapping in the stomach** are handled by a cap, not by a real stomach model with several loads (level C). This only matters for high-calorie drinks taken faster than about one every 30 minutes.
2. **Paced insulin keeps the full insulin of each round.** Jenkins measured about 50% less after sipping, so our paced insulin is on the high side.
3. **Meals are split into equal rounds** (pizza + 3 beers = 3 × (⅓ pizza + 1 beer)). In real life the food usually comes first. A per-item schedule would need its own control.
4. **First-pass metabolism at slow drinking** is not modeled. Oshima suggests slow drinking may lower the total blood alcohol (AUC), not only the peak. We stay on the cautious side.
5. **No pace beyond 4 h.** A long evening of 6 h or more is shown as 4 h. With a lower peak and the same end, 4 h is the cautious choice among the offered options.
6. Showing a link with `n=3` for beer, the "How much?" buttons list 0.3 / 0.33 / 0.5 l and none is pressed. This was already the case before this change and is not caused by pacing.
